What Is in a Second Opinion Report? A Plain Guide

2026-10-31 · 5 min read

When you ask for a written second opinion, it helps to know what you will get back. A clear report should be something you can read at your own pace, share with your doctor and use to ask better questions. This guide describes the sections a report typically contains, how to make good use of it, and what to do if it does not match what you were told before.

Key takeaways

  • A good report is written, specific to your records, and easy to follow without medical training.
  • Typical sections include a case summary, records reviewed, assessment, options, questions for your doctor and limits.
  • The report is a view based on documents. It is not an examination or a treatment plan.
  • Take it to your own doctor and discuss it, rather than acting on it alone.
  • If it disagrees with your current plan, that is a reason for a conversation, not a reason to panic.

Summary of the case

The report usually begins by restating your situation in the reviewer's words: your age and relevant background, the diagnosis you were given, and the question you asked. This is your first check. If the summary misses something important or gets a detail wrong, say so, because the rest of the report depends on it.

Records reviewed

A useful report lists what the physician actually looked at, such as clinic letters, blood tests, imaging reports or pathology results. It should also mention anything that was unreadable or missing. This section matters because an opinion is only as complete as the file behind it. If you are still gathering documents, see how to prepare medical records for a second opinion.

Assessment

Here the reviewer gives their reading of the records. They may say that the findings support the diagnosis, that something is unclear, or that more information would be needed to be sure. A careful assessment explains its reasoning in plain language and avoids overconfident statements.

Options

This part looks at the treatment or next steps you were offered and, where reasonable, other approaches that exist. It does not usually tell you to choose one. Instead it sets out what each option involves in general terms, so that you can weigh them with your treating team. Some reports also point out when waiting or monitoring is one of the options.

Questions to ask your own doctor

Many people find this the most practical section. It turns the assessment into specific questions, for example why one option was chosen over another, or whether an additional scan would change the plan. You can take this list to your next appointment. Our guide to second opinion questions before surgery offers more ideas.

Limits of the review

An honest report states what it cannot do. A review of records cannot include an examination, cannot order tests and cannot arrange treatment. It may also note where the physician had to make assumptions because information was missing. Reports that skip this section, or that make promises about outcomes, deserve caution.

How to use the report

Read it once through, then again with a pen.

1. Mark anything you do not understand. Ask the provider for clarification, or raise it with your doctor. 2. Check the summary for accuracy, and correct anything that is wrong. 3. Copy the questions into a list for your appointment. 4. Share it with your GP or specialist, and ask them to comment on it. 5. Keep it with your other records, so it is available if your care moves to another team.

What if the report disagrees?

A difference of view can feel unsettling, especially if you were hoping for a clear answer. It is worth keeping a few points in mind.

  • A difference is not proof that anyone is wrong. Doctors can reasonably read the same results differently, and the reviewer has not examined you.
  • Do not change treatment on your own. Stopping medicines or cancelling an appointment because of a report could be harmful. Speak to your doctor first.
  • Ask your treating doctor to look at the point of difference. Put it as a question: what is your view on this, and why?
  • Check that the reviewer had the same information. Sometimes a gap in the records explains the difference.
  • Consider whether more information is needed, such as an extra test or a further opinion, and discuss this with your doctor.

If your symptoms change or get worse, contact your GP, NHS 111 or emergency services in the usual way. A report is not a substitute for urgent care.

What if it agrees?

Agreement is a useful result too. It may help you feel more confident about a decision, and it can make a difficult conversation with family easier. You may still have questions, and the report can help you ask them.
