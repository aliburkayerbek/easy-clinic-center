Plastic Surgery Complications Abroad: What to Know

2026-10-31 · 5 min read

Every operation carries some risk, and plastic surgery is no exception. That is true whether you are treated in the UK or overseas. This article is for people who are thinking about plastic surgery abroad and want to understand what to ask before they decide. It does not give figures on how common any problem is, and it does not offer treatment advice. What applies to you depends on your health, the operation and the team caring for you, so your surgeon is the right person to answer those questions.

Key takeaways

  • All surgery has risks. A responsible team will explain them to you before you agree to anything.
  • Ask in advance which complications are possible in your case and how the team would handle them.
  • Find out who is responsible for your care if something goes wrong, both abroad and at home.
  • Ask for the complication and revision policy in writing, and keep every record.
  • If you are worried about your health, contact your treating team. In an urgent situation use NHS 111, call 999 or go to A&E.

Why every operation has risks

Surgery involves anaesthetic, wounds that need to heal and changes to the body. Because of this, problems can happen even when an operation is carried out carefully. Possible issues vary by procedure and by person. Your general health, medicines, previous operations and lifestyle can all matter.

Knowing this is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to make the decision with full information. A good surgical team treats risk as a normal part of the conversation. Be cautious about anyone who says an operation has no risk, or who avoids your questions about what could go wrong.

Questions to ask your surgeon before you decide

Put your questions in writing and keep the answers. You might ask:

  • What complications are possible with this operation, and which are most relevant to me?
  • How would the team recognise a problem, and what would they do about it?
  • What could make my own risk higher, for example a health condition or a medicine I take?
  • Who will be my main contact before, during and after the operation?

If you feel rushed or you are told that places will disappear unless you pay a deposit now, pause. A decision about surgery should not be hurried. Our guide to questions to ask before treatment abroad gives a wider list, and the article on what to check about plastic surgery in Turkey covers surgeon and facility questions.

Who is responsible if something goes wrong?

This is the question people most often leave until too late. Before you travel, make sure you understand who would look after you if you had a problem:

  • At the treating hospital. What care is available if a problem arises during your stay, and what happens if you need to remain longer?
  • After you return home. Which doctor will review you, and will your overseas team stay in contact and answer questions?
  • In the UK. The NHS provides emergency care, but it may not be set up to provide routine follow-up for private treatment overseas, and arrangements differ by area. Do not assume. Ask your GP in advance what they can and cannot do.

Our article on aftercare when having surgery abroad explains these points further.

Ask for the complication and revision policy in writing

Some providers have a policy for what happens if a further procedure or extra care is needed. Policies vary a great deal and may have conditions, time limits or exclusions. Do not rely on a verbal promise. Ask to see the policy in writing and read it before you pay anything. Useful questions include:

  • What does the policy cover, and what does it exclude?
  • Are hospital stay, medicines, tests and doctors' fees for additional care included or charged separately?
  • Does it apply if I need care in the UK, or only at the treating hospital?
  • Who decides whether a further procedure is needed, and how is that decided?
  • Are there time limits or conditions I must meet, such as attending follow-up appointments?

Check the same points in the quote itself. Our guide to how to read a hospital quote shows how to spot what is included and what is not.

Keep your records and tell the right people

Good records protect you and make it easier for any doctor to help you later. Keep:

  • The quote, contract, consent form and any written policy.
  • Your operation note, discharge summary and a list of any medicines you were given.
  • Written aftercare instructions from the surgical team.
  • Contact details for your overseas team and a record of your messages with them.

Tell your GP that you have had surgery abroad and share these documents with them. If you have travel insurance, read what it covers before you go, because many policies exclude planned treatment and related complications. Our article on medical travel insurance in the UK explains what to look for.

When to seek urgent help

Follow the written instructions your surgical team gives you about warning signs, because they are specific to your operation. In general:

  • If you feel unwell or are worried, contact your treating team or your GP.
  • For urgent medical advice in the UK, call NHS 111.
  • In an emergency, such as severe breathlessness, chest pain or heavy bleeding, call 999 or go to A&E.

Tell emergency staff that you have recently had surgery abroad and show them your documents.
