Second Opinion Before Surgery: When and How to Ask

2026-10-31 · 4 min read

Being told you need an operation can feel like a lot to take in. Many people want a second opinion before surgery but worry it will seem rude, or they are unsure how to go about it. It is a normal request, and most surgeons are used to it. This guide explains when it is worth doing, how to ask a useful question, what to send, and how to use the answer.

Key takeaways

  • A second opinion before surgery is a review of your records by an independent specialist.
  • It is most useful for major, complex, irreversible or elective operations.
  • A focused question gets a more useful answer than "is this right?".
  • Complete, recent records matter more than a long list of documents.
  • The report helps you prepare for a conversation. It does not replace your surgeon.

When it is worth getting one

Not every operation needs a second view. It is often worth considering when:

  • The surgery is major or complex, or has a long recovery.
  • The operation cannot be undone, or carries significant risks.
  • The surgery is elective, meaning you have time to decide.
  • You were offered only one option, and you would like to understand alternatives such as medication, physiotherapy, monitoring or a different technique.
  • Different clinicians have said different things.
  • You feel unsure, even though you cannot say exactly why.

If your situation is urgent, for example an emergency or a condition where delay could cause harm, follow the advice of your treating team and call NHS 111 or 999 as appropriate. A second opinion is for decisions where there is time to think, and it should never hold up urgent care.

How to frame your question

A broad request such as "please check my diagnosis" often gets a broad answer. A clear question helps the reviewing physician focus. Consider writing two or three sentences covering:

1. What you were told. For example, the diagnosis and the operation proposed. 2. What you want to know. Is surgery necessary now? Are there other reasonable options? What are the main risks in my situation? 3. Anything particular about you. Other conditions, medication, previous operations, allergies or reasons you are hesitant.

Examples of useful questions include "Do my scans support the need for this operation?", "Would waiting and monitoring be a reasonable alternative?" and "Is the proposed approach standard for my condition, and what should I ask the surgeon about it?"

What to send

The reviewing physician can only comment on what they can see. Typically you will want to gather:

  • Clinic letters and the surgeon's recommendation, including any plan or consent information you have been given.
  • Imaging reports, and the images themselves if you can obtain them (scans on disc or via a link).
  • Blood tests and other results relevant to the condition.
  • Pathology or biopsy reports, if there are any.
  • A list of current medication, allergies and previous surgery.

Using the report with your surgeon

Once you have the report, treat it as a basis for discussion rather than a verdict.

  • Read it calmly, and note anything you do not understand.
  • Book time to discuss it with your NHS or private surgeon, or your GP. You can say simply that you sought another view and would like to talk it through.
  • Take specific questions. Ask why one approach was chosen over another, what the risks and recovery look like, and what happens if you wait.
  • Expect different outcomes. The report may agree with your surgeon, which can bring reassurance. It may raise another option or ask for further tests. Either way, your treating team knows your full history and examines you in person.
  • Make your own decision. After the discussion, it is your choice whether to proceed, wait or seek further advice.
