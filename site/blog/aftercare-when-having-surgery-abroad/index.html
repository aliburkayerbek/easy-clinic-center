Aftercare After Surgery Abroad: A Planning Guide for UK Patients

2026-10-31 · 5 min read

Most of the attention before surgery abroad goes on the operation itself. Yet what happens afterwards matters just as much, because recovery continues long after you have left the hospital. This guide is for UK patients who want to plan aftercare in advance. It cannot cover every procedure, and your surgeon's instructions always come first.

Key takeaways

  • Ask about aftercare before you book, and get the answers in writing.
  • Do not fly home until the treating surgeon says it is safe for your procedure.
  • Know who to contact abroad and at home, and have a plan for complications.
  • The NHS provides emergency care, but routine follow-up for private treatment abroad is not guaranteed.
  • Carry full documents and keep copies of everything.

Plan follow-up before you travel

The questions to ask are practical ones.

  • What review will I have before I leave? Ask for a final check by the surgeon, not only a nurse or coordinator.
  • How long should I stay? The right length depends on the operation. A rushed departure raises the chance of problems on the journey.
  • What follow-up will happen after I go home? For example, remote check-ins, photographs, or a video review at set points.
  • Who removes stitches or dressings, and where? If this will need to happen in the UK, find out who will do it before you travel.
  • What will I need at home? Medication, dressings, compression wear or equipment should be arranged in advance.

Our list of questions to ask before treatment abroad includes more on what to settle before committing. If you are travelling to Turkey, the UK checklist for planning a trip for treatment covers practical details such as accommodation and time off.

Flying home safely

Surgery and flying can interact. After some operations, long periods of sitting and changes in cabin pressure can raise risks such as blood clots or discomfort, and the safe timing varies by procedure and by person.

  • Ask your surgeon for a specific answer on when you can fly, and ask for it in writing.
  • Do not book a non-flexible ticket that pushes you to travel early. A changeable ticket gives you room if recovery is slower than planned.
  • Ask about travel insurance. Standard policies often exclude planned treatment abroad or its complications, so read the terms and speak to the insurer before you go.
  • Follow advice on movement, hydration and compression, if your surgeon recommends them.
  • Arrange help at the airport and at home for luggage and transport, so you are not lifting or straining.
  • Keep medication and key documents in hand luggage.

Know who to call

Decide in advance who you will contact for different situations, and write the details down.

1. The treating team abroad. Ask for a named contact and a number or messaging route that is monitored, and what hours it covers. 2. Your coordinator or consultancy, if you use one, who may help with communication and records. 3. Your GP. Let your GP know you have had surgery, ideally before you travel or soon after you return, so they can help with routine concerns. 4. NHS 111 for urgent advice when you are not sure what to do. 5. 999 or A&E for emergencies such as chest pain, difficulty breathing, heavy bleeding, or signs of a blood clot like a swollen, painful leg.

The role of the NHS

This is often misunderstood, so it is worth stating carefully. The NHS will provide emergency treatment to anyone who needs it. For non-urgent follow-up after private treatment abroad, the position is less clear. Some GPs and local services will help with simple things such as a check-up or a dressing, while others may be limited in what they can take on, particularly when they do not have full information about the operation. Do not assume that routine aftercare will be arranged for you. Ask in advance and, where possible, agree a plan with your GP before you travel.

Have a complications plan

Most people recover without serious trouble, but a plan lets you act quickly if they do not.

  • Ask the surgeon which symptoms are expected and which are warning signs, such as rising pain, fever, spreading redness, discharge or unusual swelling.
  • Agree what to do if a warning sign appears: who to message first, and when to go straight to A&E.
  • Know what the arrangement is if you need further treatment. Who pays, where would it take place, and could it happen in the UK?
  • Take a photograph of the area as it looks when you leave, to help describe any changes later.

Documents to carry

Gather these in a folder, on paper and digitally:

  • Operation or discharge summary, stating what was done, the date and the surgeon.
  • Medication list, with doses and instructions.
  • Implant or device details, where relevant, such as brand and batch information.
  • Test and imaging results, including copies of scans.
  • Written aftercare instructions and the treating team's contact details.
  • Your own medical history and allergy information.
  • Insurance documents and receipts.
