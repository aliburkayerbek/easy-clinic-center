What to Do After a Second Opinion: Your Next Steps

2026-10-31 · 5 min read

Receiving a second opinion report can bring relief, new questions, or both. It is easy to feel that you must act straight away, but you do not. The report is information to help you and your own doctors make a decision, not a deadline. This article explains what to do after a second opinion: how to read the report, who to share it with, what to do whether it agrees or differs from your first doctor, and how to decide on next steps without being rushed. It does not give medical advice, and decisions about your care belong with you and your own doctor.

Key takeaways

  • Read the report slowly, more than once, and note anything you do not understand.
  • Take it to your own doctor, who knows your full history and can discuss what it means for you.
  • Whether the report agrees or differs with your first opinion, you can ask questions and take time.
  • Do not rush into treatment or cancel planned care without talking to your doctor first.
  • Keep the report, your records and your notes together for future appointments.

Reading the report

Set aside quiet time to read the report. A second read helps the details settle. As you read:

  • Underline or note any medical terms you do not know, and look them up in reliable sources or ask your doctor.
  • Remember that the report reflects only the records you sent.
  • Note any limits the report mentions, such as information that was missing or tests that might add clarity.
  • Write down your questions as they come up.

Our article on what to expect from a second opinion report describes how reports are usually structured, if you want a guide to the sections.

What to take to your own doctor

Your own GP or specialist knows you, can examine you and has access to your full history. That makes them the right person to help you decide what to do. Consider taking:

  • the second opinion report, printed or on your phone
  • your original records, or the list of what was sent for review
  • a short list of questions and concerns
  • a note of any medicines, symptoms or changes since the report was written
  • a relative or friend, if you would like support in the appointment

If your appointment time is short, tell the surgery in advance that you would like to discuss a report, so they can allow enough time.

If the report agrees with your first opinion

Agreement can be reassuring, but you may still want to understand:

  • why both doctors reached the same view
  • what the plan involves, step by step
  • the risks, the alternatives and what happens if you wait
  • what you can do for yourself in the meantime, if your doctor advises it

The decision is still yours, made with your own team.

If the report differs

A difference does not mean that one doctor is right and the other wrong. Medicine often involves more than one reasonable approach, and doctors can weigh the same information differently or have access to different details. If the report differs from your first opinion:

  • Do not dismiss either opinion straight away.
  • Ask your own doctor to look at both and explain the differences in plain language.
  • Ask what each approach would mean in practice, including risks, recovery and follow-up.
  • Ask whether any further tests or information might help settle the question.
  • Consider whether your records were complete when each opinion was given.

Your doctor may suggest a further review. Our guide on questions to ask for a second opinion before surgery lists helpful prompts that work well here too.

Questions for next steps

Bring a list to your appointment. Some useful questions are:

  • What do you think is the best next step for me, and why?
  • Are there other options I should consider?
  • What are the risks and benefits of each?
  • What happens if I wait, and how long is it reasonable to wait?
  • Is there anything in the report that you would like to check or clarify?
  • Which further tests, if any, would be useful?

Write down the answers, as details are easy to forget.

Deciding whether to explore treatment, and not rushing

Some people, after a second opinion, decide to look at treatment options, including abroad. That is a personal decision, and there is no need to make it quickly. If you do wish to explore it:

  • Talk to your own doctor first, particularly about timing and any follow-up you would need in the UK.
  • Ask for any plan or quote in writing, and read it away from any sales conversation.
  • Compare like with like. Our guide on how to read a hospital quote explains what to look for.
  • Be wary of anyone who pressures you to decide or pay quickly.

It is also perfectly reasonable to decide to do nothing new, to stay with your current plan, or to wait and see. The right choice depends on your health, your circumstances and your doctor's advice.

Keeping your records

Keep everything together in one place: the second opinion report, your original records, notes of conversations and any letters from your doctors. Date your notes, so the file is ready if you need another review later.
