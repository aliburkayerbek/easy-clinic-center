Second Opinion vs NHS Referral: Key Differences

2026-11-01 · 5 min read

If you have been given a diagnosis or a treatment recommendation, you may wonder whether to ask for another view. In the UK there is more than one way to do that. This guide compares a second opinion arranged through the NHS with an online written review of your records, explains how they can work together, and is honest about what an online review cannot do.

Key takeaways

  • An NHS referral and an online record review are different routes with different strengths.
  • An NHS referral can include examination, tests and a treating relationship. A record review cannot.
  • An online review is based only on the documents you send, so a complete file matters.
  • The two are not rivals. Many people use a written report to prepare for a conversation with their NHS team.
  • It is sensible to tell your GP or consultant that you have sought another view.

The NHS routes to a second opinion

In general terms, people who want another medical view through the NHS usually start with a conversation. You can raise it with your GP or with the hospital specialist who is looking after you, and explain what you are unsure about. Your doctor may agree to refer you to another specialist, may suggest that your case is discussed with colleagues, or may explain why they think the current plan is right.

Whether a referral is made is a clinical decision, and the process differs between hospitals and areas. We do not describe local rules here, so ask your own team how it works where you live, and whether there is a patient advice service at your hospital that can explain it.

Some points are worth knowing in advance:

  • An NHS second opinion is normally given by a clinician who can see your full NHS record and, where needed, examine you.
  • The new specialist may order further tests or repeat scans before giving a view.
  • The outcome is usually discussed with you and fed back to your existing team, which can make it easier to act on.

How an online physician review differs

An online second opinion works in a different way. You send your records, such as clinic letters, test results, scan reports and a short description of your question. A licensed specialist physician reads them and writes a report. You do not travel, and there is no examination.

The main differences, in plain terms:

  • Access to you. An NHS specialist can examine you. An online reviewer works from paper and images only.
  • Access to your record. The NHS clinician can see the whole NHS file. An online reviewer sees only what you send.
  • Format. An online review produces a written report you keep. An NHS opinion may be given in clinic, by letter, or through discussion between doctors.
  • Convenience. An online review can be done from home, which may suit people who find it hard to attend appointments.
  • Role in your care. The NHS team can act on its own conclusion by arranging treatment. An online reviewer cannot.

How the two can work together

Seeing the options as either-or is not helpful. For many people, a written report is a way of preparing, not replacing, a conversation with the NHS.

  • It can help you understand a diagnosis in plain language before an appointment.
  • It can give you specific questions to ask, rather than a general feeling that you are unsure.
  • If it agrees with your current plan, it may help you feel more settled.
  • If it raises a different point, you can put that to your NHS team and ask them to consider it.

What an online review cannot do

It is important to be clear about limits.

  • It cannot examine you, so it cannot replace a clinic visit.
  • It cannot order tests, prescribe medicines or arrange treatment on the NHS.
  • It cannot change your NHS care plan. Only your treating team can do that.
  • It cannot guarantee that a diagnosis is wrong, or that another approach would work better.
  • It is only as good as the records provided. Missing scans or unclear letters limit what can be said.

Because of these limits, an online report should be treated as an informed view to discuss with your doctors, not as an instruction to change treatment. Never stop or alter medicines or a treatment plan because of a report without speaking to your own doctor first. How to tell your NHS team

Some people worry that mentioning a second opinion will upset their doctor. In practice, most clinicians are used to the question, and being open helps them look after you.

1. Choose a calm moment, such as a planned appointment, rather than the end of a busy clinic. 2. Be straightforward. You might say that you would like to understand your options better and have asked for an independent review of your records. 3. Share the report if you are comfortable doing so, and ask your doctor to comment on any differences. 4. Ask questions, not for a verdict. For example, why was this option chosen over another, and what would change the plan? 5. Keep a copy of anything you receive, and note what was said.

If there is any urgency or your symptoms change, contact your GP, NHS 111 or emergency services as you normally would. Do not wait for a second opinion.
