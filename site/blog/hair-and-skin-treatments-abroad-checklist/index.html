Hair and Skin Treatments Abroad: A UK Checklist

2026-10-31 · 5 min read

Hair and skin treatments are often presented as simple and low-risk. In reality, they are procedures carried out on your body and deserve careful questions. This checklist is for UK patients who are thinking about a hair transplant or a skin treatment abroad, including in Turkey. It does not recommend any treatment or provider.

Key takeaways

  • Ask for the name and qualifications of the person who will actually perform the procedure, and get them in writing.
  • A proper medical assessment should come before any plan or price.
  • You should be told about risks and limits as well as benefits, and be given time to decide.
  • Get a written plan and quote that you can read at home, away from any sales conversation.
  • Ask for aftercare and sun and activity advice from the treating clinician, and plan your UK follow-up before you travel.

Who will perform the procedure

This is the most important question, and it is worth asking directly. In some places, different parts of a hair or skin procedure may be carried out by different people. Ask:

  • Who will carry out each stage of the treatment, from the first assessment to the final step?
  • What is their professional qualification, and what is their specialty?
  • Who will be present throughout, and who is responsible if something goes wrong?
  • Can I have the answers in writing before I travel?

Vague replies, or an unwillingness to name the people involved, are reasons to pause. Our article on questions to ask before treatment abroad has a wider list you can adapt.

Medical assessment before any plan

Hair loss and skin problems can have many causes, some linked to other conditions or medicines. A sensible provider will want to understand your situation first. Expect questions about:

  • your medical history, including long-term conditions and allergies
  • medicines and supplements you take now or have taken recently
  • previous treatments, and how your skin or scalp responded
  • any skin conditions, scarring tendencies or healing problems

If a price arrives before anyone has asked about your health, treat that as a warning sign. It is reasonable to ask whether the treatment suits you at all, and what the alternatives are, including doing nothing for now.

Risks, limits and what you should be told

Every procedure has risks and limits, even when it is common. You should be given a balanced explanation, not only the benefits. Ask the clinician to explain, in plain language:

  • the possible side effects and complications, and how common or rare they are considered to be
  • what the treatment can and cannot realistically do for your particular case
  • whether more than one session may be needed
  • what would make you unsuitable, or make the team decide not to go ahead
  • what happens if you are unhappy with the outcome or develop a problem

Be cautious about any provider who talks only about guaranteed or perfect outcomes. Results vary between people, and an honest clinician will say so. Do not sign consent forms you have not read and understood. For more on risks in general, see our article on plastic surgery complications and what to know.

A written plan and quote

Ask for a written plan and a written quote that you can read at home. It should set out:

  • the exact procedure and the hospital or clinic where it will take place
  • the name of the treating doctor or clinician
  • what is included, and what is not
  • payment terms, and cancellation and refund conditions
  • what aftercare and follow-up are included

We coordinate treatment at partner hospitals and give written quotes as Option A (all-inclusive: treatment, flights, accommodation and transfers) or Option B (treatment only), so you can see exactly what is covered.

Aftercare, sun and activity advice

What you do after a hair or skin treatment can affect how you heal, so do not leave this until the last day. Ask the treating clinician, not a salesperson, for written advice on:

  • how to care for the treated area, and what to avoid touching or using
  • sun exposure and protection, including when you travel home and in the weeks afterwards
  • exercise, swimming, saunas, hats and other everyday activities
  • which medicines or products to use, and which to avoid
  • signs that mean you should seek help, and who to contact

If you are flying home soon after treatment, ask whether that affects anything. Our article on aftercare when having surgery abroad covers how to plan recovery.

Follow-up in the UK

Plan follow-up before you go. Ask the overseas team to give you a letter or summary describing what was done, the products and medicines used, and any instructions.

When you are home, consider telling your GP what you had done. Check in advance what your GP or local clinic can do, because arrangements differ. Ask the overseas team how you can reach them after you leave, and how quickly they usually reply. If you notice something that worries you, such as increasing pain, spreading redness, discharge, fever or anything unexpected, contact a healthcare professional promptly rather than waiting.
