Medical Records for a Second Opinion: A Practical UK Checklist

2026-10-18 · 4 min read

A second opinion is only as useful as the records behind it. This applies whether the reviewing doctor is in the UK or in Türkiye. A specialist who sees complete, clear and well-ordered documents can give a more focused answer than one who has to work around gaps. This guide explains what to collect, how to request it from the NHS or a private provider, and how to put it together before you send it.

Key takeaways

  • Gather reports, imaging, lab results, a medication list and a short timeline.
  • Ask for scans on disc or in DICOM format, not only the written report.
  • Never send face photos. They are not required, ever.
  • In the UK you can request your own records through a subject access request.
  • A clear question helps the reviewing physician focus on what matters to you.

The core checklist

Not every item will apply to you. Collect what exists and note anything you cannot find.

  • Clinic letters and discharge summaries from your GP, consultant or hospital.
  • Diagnosis reports, including histology or pathology reports if a sample was taken.
  • Operation notes, if you have had surgery related to the problem.
  • Treatment plans or letters that describe what has been proposed.
  • Previous test results that show how your condition has changed over time.

Recent documents matter most, but older ones can be valuable when they show a trend or a previous treatment.

Imaging: CDs, DICOM and reports

Scans such as MRI, CT, ultrasound, X-ray and PET are a common source of delay. The written radiology report is helpful, but the specialist often needs to see the images themselves.

  • Request the images on a CD or in DICOM format. DICOM is the standard file type for medical imaging.
  • Keep the written report with the images, since the two belong together.
  • Check the dates, so the reviewer knows which scan is the most recent.
  • If you only have a link or portal access, ask the provider how the images can be exported.

Do not convert scans into screenshots or phone photographs unless you have no alternative, because image quality is lost.

Laboratory results

Include blood tests, urine tests, tumour markers or any other lab results connected to your condition. If you have a series of results over several months, send the whole series rather than a single value. A trend often says more than one number.

Medication list and allergies

Write down every medicine you take, including:

  • The name, dose and how often you take it.
  • Over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and supplements.
  • Any medicines you have stopped, and why.
  • Known allergies or bad reactions.

A recent printout from your GP surgery is ideal, but a careful handwritten list is fine.

A simple timeline

A one-page timeline helps the reviewer understand your story quickly. Keep it short and factual:

1. When symptoms began. 2. When you saw each doctor and what was found. 3. Which tests and treatments you have had, and the results. 4. What has been recommended and what you are deciding.

Finish with your question, for example: "Is this operation necessary, and are there alternatives?"

Photos: the rule

Some conditions benefit from clinical photos, but there is one firm rule: no face photos, ever. If a photo is relevant to your condition, send only the affected area, and only if it is clearly useful. When in doubt, leave photos out and describe the problem in words. Do not send identification documents or payment details with your records.

How to request your records in the UK

You have a legal right to see the personal data held about you. Under the UK Data Protection Act and UK GDPR, you can make a subject access request to your GP surgery, NHS trust or private provider.

  • Make the request in writing, by email or letter, to the provider's data protection or medical records team.
  • Say clearly which records you want and the dates they cover.
  • Providers usually respond within one month, which is the standard UK GDPR period.
  • For scans, ask specifically for the images in DICOM format or on disc as well as the report.

Some GP surgeries also let you view parts of your record through the NHS App. Your own doctor's team can tell you what is available. If a provider refuses or delays, you can ask them to explain, and the Information Commissioner's Office offers guidance on your rights.

Before you send

  • Check that every page is readable and the right way up.
  • Put documents in date order, newest first.
  • Name files clearly, for example "MRI report March 2026".
  • Remove anything unrelated to your condition.
