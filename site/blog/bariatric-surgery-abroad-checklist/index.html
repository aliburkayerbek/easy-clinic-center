Weight Loss Surgery Abroad: A Checklist for UK Patients

2026-10-31 · 5 min read

Weight loss surgery, also called bariatric surgery, changes how your digestive system works and is not a quick fix. Some UK patients consider having it abroad, often because of waiting times or cost. This checklist is for people who are thinking about it carefully. It does not say whether surgery is right for you and gives no figures for outcomes, because results and risks depend on the person, the operation and the care before and afterwards.

Key takeaways

  • Surgery should follow a proper medical assessment, not a sales conversation.
  • Gather your records, because the surgical team needs your full history to judge suitability.
  • A second opinion can check whether the proposed operation fits your situation.
  • Aftercare is lifelong. Ask who will provide nutrition and medical follow-up, in the UK and at the treating hospital.
  • Insist on a written plan and quote, and let the surgical team decide when travel is appropriate.

Start with medical suitability

Whether bariatric surgery is appropriate depends on your health, your history and how other approaches have worked for you. Before anything is booked, you should expect an assessment by a qualified team, not only a form and photographs. Questions to ask include:

  • Who will assess me, and which specialists are involved, such as the surgeon, a physician and a dietitian?
  • What tests will be done, and will I have a chance to discuss the results?
  • Which operation is proposed and why? Are there alternatives, including non-surgical options?
  • What conditions or medicines of mine need to be considered, such as diabetes, heart or breathing problems, or blood thinners?
  • Is a psychological or eating-pattern assessment part of the process?
  • What would make the team decide that I should not have surgery?

A team that is willing to say no, or to ask you to wait, is behaving responsibly. Be cautious about any service that confirms you are suitable before looking at your history.

Why your records and a second opinion matter

Bariatric surgery is planned around your medical background, so complete records matter. Gather recent blood tests, clinic letters, medication lists, any previous operations, scans, and notes from your GP or hospital doctors. Our guide to how to prepare medical records for a second opinion explains how to organise them.

A second opinion gives you an independent review of the proposal before you commit. A licensed specialist physician looks at your records and writes a report, which may highlight missing tests, raise questions about the proposed operation or simply confirm that the plan is reasonable. It is based on documents, so it does not replace an examination by the surgical team. Our article on what to expect from a second opinion report describes what the report covers.

Aftercare is not optional

Surgery is the start of a long process. After most bariatric operations, people need to change how and what they eat, and many need regular monitoring and nutritional supplements. This support is usually described as lifelong, and the arrangements should be clear before you travel.

Ask the team:

  • Which follow-up is included, for how long, and in what form?
  • Will there be a multidisciplinary team, with dietitian and physician support as well as the surgeon?
  • What diet and supplement advice will I receive in writing, and who adjusts it over time?
  • Which blood tests will I need in the future, and how often?
  • What symptoms should make me contact someone urgently?

Do not rely on general internet advice for any of this. The treating team should give you instructions that fit your operation.

Who coordinates care in the UK?

This is the question many people overlook. Once you are home, someone has to be able to monitor you, order tests and respond if you are unwell. Speak to your GP before travelling if you can, so you know what they are able and willing to do. NHS services are not obliged to provide the same follow-up that a private or overseas programme would, and arrangements differ by area, so do not assume.

Consider:

  • Whether a UK doctor, dietitian or private service has agreed to provide follow-up.
  • How your overseas team will send your operation note, discharge summary and test results to your GP.
  • Who you will call if you have a problem out of hours. For urgent advice you can use NHS 111, and in an emergency call 999 or go to A&E.
  • What your travel insurance covers, since many policies exclude planned treatment abroad and related complications.

Our guide to aftercare when having surgery abroad explains the wider issues.

Insist on a written plan and quote

Before you pay a deposit, ask for the plan in writing. It should state the proposed operation, the hospital, the surgeon, the assessments required and what happens if the team decides not to operate. It should also say what the price includes and excludes, for example tests, hospital stay, medicines, accommodation, transfers and follow-up, and what happens if you need extra care.

Let the surgical team decide on travel timing

Do not book fixed flights or time off work until the surgical team has told you what is needed. The assessment, the operation date, the review before you leave and the advice about when you can travel home are medical decisions made for your case. Choose flexible tickets where you can, avoid tight schedules, and ask for the fly-home advice in writing. Plan for someone to be with you, and for time away from your usual routine, but take the details from your surgeon rather than from general guides.
