When people plan treatment abroad, insurance is often an afterthought. They assume the travel policy they buy for holidays will respond if something goes wrong. That assumption can be a costly one. This guide explains why travel insurance and treatment abroad do not always fit together, what to ask before you book, and which documents to keep. It is general information only. We do not recommend any insurer or product, and the terms of your own policy are what count.

## Key takeaways

- Standard travel insurance often excludes planned treatment, but policies differ, so read your own wording.
- Do not rely on a sales summary. Ask the insurer in writing whether your planned treatment and any complications are covered.
- Tell the insurer about your medical conditions. Failing to do so can cause problems later.
- Keep your policy, correspondence and treatment papers together in one place.
- Insurance is only one part of your safety net. Aftercare planning and a clear written quote matter too.

## Why standard travel insurance may not apply

Many travel policies are designed for unplanned events during a trip, such as sudden illness, an accident, lost luggage or a cancelled flight. Travelling specifically to have treatment is a different situation, because the need for care is known in advance.

For that reason, many policies exclude planned treatment, or exclude cover for medical problems that arise from it. Some may exclude cosmetic procedures, or complications linked to a procedure you chose to have abroad. Others may treat it differently. We cannot say what your policy contains, and wording varies between insurers and between policy levels. The only reliable way to find out is to read your documents and ask the insurer directly.

Also be careful with credit card or bank account travel cover, and with any policy that came bundled with another product. These often have their own exclusions and limits, and the same questions apply.

## Read the policy wording, not only the summary

Policy documents are long, but a few sections are worth reading closely before you travel.

- **Exclusions.** Look for any mention of planned or elective treatment, cosmetic surgery, dental work or travelling for medical purposes.
- **Definitions.** Check how the policy defines terms such as pre-existing medical condition, medical treatment and emergency.
- **Medical conditions.** See what you must declare, and what happens if you do not.
- **Limits and excess.** Note the maximum payable for medical costs and for bringing someone home, and any excess you would pay.
- **Conditions.** Some policies require you to contact an assistance line before arranging care. Check whether that applies.
- **Cancellation and curtailment.** Find out whether the policy covers cancelling or cutting short a trip, and for which reasons.

If the wording is unclear, do not guess. Ask the insurer to explain it in writing.

## Questions to ask the insurer

Because this is a high-stakes decision, ask directly and keep the replies. You might ask:

1. Does this policy cover travel where the purpose is planned medical or dental treatment?
2. If not, is there a policy, extension or specialist product from you that does?
3. If treatment goes ahead, are complications arising from it covered, and for how long after the treatment?
4. Does the answer differ between a planned procedure and an emergency that is unrelated to it?
5. Are additional costs covered, such as an extended stay, extra accommodation, changed flights or a companion's expenses?
6. Is medical repatriation covered, and under what conditions?
7. Which of my medical conditions must I declare, and will that change the cover or the terms?
8. What documents would I need to support a claim?

Ask for answers by email or letter rather than relying on a phone call, or note the date, time and name of the person you spoke to. Make sure your question describes the actual treatment and destination. A reply to a vague question may not protect you.

## Documents to keep

Good records help with insurers, with your GP and with any later review of your care. Keep, in paper and digital form:

- Your policy schedule and full policy wording, and the written replies from the insurer.
- Your written quote and booking confirmations, including what is and is not included in the price.
- Records of your consent, treatment plan and any changes to it.
- Discharge papers, operation or procedure notes, medicine lists and test results.
- Receipts and invoices for treatment, travel, accommodation and medicines.
- Contact details for the treating clinic and the insurer's assistance line.

## Insurance is only one part of the plan

Even where cover is available, it does not replace careful planning. Before you travel, make sure you have a clear written plan and quote, an agreed follow-up arrangement, and a way to contact the treating team. Our list of [questions to ask before treatment abroad](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-treatment-abroad/) and our [checklist for planning a trip to Turkey for treatment](/blog/planning-a-trip-to-turkey-for-treatment-uk-checklist/) can help you cover the practical points.

Also speak to your GP, since they may be asked to help with follow-up and it is better to know their position in advance. Remember that NHS services are not obliged to provide the same follow-up that an overseas programme might, and arrangements differ by area. For urgent advice in the UK you can use NHS 111, and in an emergency call 999 or go to A&E.
