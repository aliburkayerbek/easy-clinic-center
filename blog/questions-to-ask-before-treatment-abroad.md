Travelling for treatment can be a sensible choice for some people, but it deserves more preparation than a normal holiday booking. When your surgeon and your aftercare team are in different countries, small gaps in planning can become big problems. This checklist is deliberately neutral. It applies to any country and any provider, including us, and the answers should be in writing.

## Key takeaways

- Verify qualifications and accreditation yourself, from independent sources.
- Ask for an itemised written quote that states what is and is not included.
- Know the plan for complications and aftercare before you travel.
- Ask when it is safe to fly home, and check your insurance.
- Tell your GP what you are planning.

## 1. The surgeon and the team

Do not rely on a brochure or a website to tell you who will treat you. Ask:

- What is the surgeon's full name, specialty and training?
- Are they registered with the relevant medical authority in their country?
- How often do they perform the procedure you are considering?
- Will the same surgeon operate and see you afterwards, or will someone else?
- Who is the anaesthetist, and what qualifications do they hold?

Ask for the answers in writing. A reputable provider will not mind being asked.

## 2. The hospital and its accreditation

Hospitals often display logos and certificates. Treat these as claims to check rather than facts to accept.

- Ask which accreditations the hospital holds and which body awarded them.
- **Verify this independently** on the accrediting body's own website or with the relevant health authority.
- Ask whether the hospital has intensive care facilities and what happens if you need urgent help during or after surgery.
- Ask whether the operation will take place in that hospital or in a separate clinic.

## 3. An itemised written quote

A clear quote protects you from surprises. It should state:

- The exact procedure and any related fees (surgeon, anaesthesia, theatre, medication, hospital stay).
- What is included and what is not, such as tests, follow-up visits and dressings.
- What happens to the price if the plan changes, or if you need an extra night.
- Payment terms, refunds and cancellation rules.

If travel is bundled in, ask for flights, hotel and transfers to be listed separately so you can compare. ## 4. Risks, complications and aftercare

Every operation carries risk. A good provider talks about this openly.

- What are the main risks of this procedure for someone with my history?
- What is the plan if there is a complication while I am still abroad?
- What happens if a problem appears after I have returned to the UK?
- Who pays for corrective treatment, and where would it take place?
- How do I contact the team out of hours, and in which language?

Be cautious about any provider who describes surgery as risk-free or avoids these questions.

## 5. Travelling after surgery

Flying too soon after some operations can raise the risk of problems such as blood clots. Ask the surgeon in advance:

- How long must I stay before it is safe to fly home?
- Are there restrictions on lifting, bathing or sitting for long periods?
- Do I need a companion with me?
- When will the stitches or dressings be checked, and by whom?

Plan to stay for as long as the surgeon advises, and allow extra time in case of delays.

## 6. Insurance

Standard travel insurance may not cover complications from planned treatment abroad. Read the policy carefully and ask the insurer directly. Specialist medical travel cover exists, but check the exclusions, the limits and whether complications after returning home are covered.

## 7. Tell your GP

Let your GP know about your plans, and ask whether any tests or medication changes are needed beforehand. After you return, your GP or NHS team can help with routine follow-up, though they may not be able to take on responsibility for complications from surgery done elsewhere. It helps to bring home a full set of records, including the operation notes and discharge summary.

## 8. Get a second opinion first

Before committing, have an independent specialist review your diagnosis and the proposed treatment. It can confirm that the plan makes sense or raise questions worth asking. Read [what a second medical opinion involves](/blog/what-is-a-second-medical-opinion/) to see how this works.

## Official guidance

The NHS publishes its own advice on this subject. Read the [NHS page on going abroad for treatment](https://www.nhs.uk/using-the-nhs/healthcare-abroad/going-abroad-for-treatment/) before you decide.
