Hearing that you may have cancer is difficult, and it is natural to feel overwhelmed. Many people want to understand what they have been told and to hear a second medical view. Asking for one is a normal step and your right as a patient. This article explains how a second opinion works, which records to gather and which questions to ask. It does not interpret any result or say what treatment you need, because that must come from your own doctors who know your full situation.

## Key takeaways

- A second opinion is a normal and accepted part of care. Most doctors are used to patients asking for one.
- It does not replace your treatment or your treating team. Keep your appointments and follow their advice.
- Gather your pathology and biopsy reports, imaging reports and scans, clinic letters and medication list.
- Write your questions down and bring someone with you if you can.
- If you feel unwell or have urgent symptoms, contact your treating team or NHS 111 / 999.

## A second opinion is a right and a normal step

You are allowed to ask another doctor to look at your diagnosis and the plan proposed. Asking does not mean you distrust your team. It is a way of making sure you understand your situation and feel confident about the next steps. In the UK you can ask your GP or hospital doctor about it, and you can also seek a review privately.

A second opinion is a further view of the same information. It may agree with the current plan, which many people find reassuring, or it may raise points for you to discuss with your own specialist. Either way, the decisions stay with you and your treating team. Our guide on [second opinions before surgery](/blog/second-opinion-before-surgery-questions/) explains the wider picture, and our article on [second opinion versus NHS referral](/blog/second-opinion-vs-nhs-referral/) describes how the two differ.

## What it does not do

Be clear about its limits. A second opinion based on documents cannot examine you, and it cannot replace treatment, tests or urgent care. It should never be a reason to delay what your treating team has advised. If your team has given you appointment dates, keep them while any review is under way. If you are unsure whether a second opinion would affect your timetable, ask your treating team before you do anything, and let them know what you plan.

## Records to gather

A review is only as good as the information it is based on. Try to collect:

- **Pathology and biopsy reports.** The full written laboratory report, not only a summary.
- **Imaging reports and scans.** Written reports from any scan, together with the images on disc or in a file if you can obtain them.
- **Clinic letters.** Letters from your GP, hospital doctors and any specialists.
- **Blood test results,** with dates.
- **Medication list.** Everything you take, with doses, including supplements and over-the-counter medicines.
- **Previous operations and relevant history.** Including allergies and other conditions you live with.
- **Any treatment plan or consent forms** you have been given.

Your GP surgery or hospital can usually provide copies, and in the UK you have a right to see your records. Ask for them in electronic form if possible. Put the files in date order and name them clearly. Our guide on [how to prepare medical records for a second opinion](/blog/how-to-prepare-medical-records-for-a-second-opinion/) has practical tips.

## Questions to ask a second physician

Appointments can be hard to absorb, so write your questions in advance. You might ask:

- Do you have all the information needed to give a view, or is anything missing?
- Do you agree with the diagnosis as it has been explained to me, and are there points you would ask my team to clarify?
- Does the proposed plan look reasonable, and are there other options I should ask my team about?
- Are there further tests that could be useful, and who should arrange them?
- What questions should I take back to my own specialist?
- How should I explain this review to my treating team?

Keep in mind that a physician working from documents will not be able to answer every question, and some can only be answered by those who examine you. Our article on [what to expect from a second opinion report](/blog/what-to-expect-second-opinion-report/) shows how the written feedback is usually structured.

## Telling your treating team

Many people worry about how their doctors will react. In practice, being open is usually the best approach. You can say something simple, such as: "I would like to get a second view so that I fully understand my options. Can you let me have copies of my records?" Teams deal with this request often and generally want you to feel informed.

Practical points:

- Ask for copies of your reports and scans early, since they can take time to arrive.
- Tell your team if you decide to seek a review, and keep them informed of any outcome.
- Take the second opinion report to your next appointment and go through it with your own specialist.
- Do not stop or change any treatment or medicine unless your treating team has told you to.

## Looking after yourself

It is common to feel anxious, tired or unsure where to turn. You do not have to deal with this on your own. Ask a relative or friend to come to appointments and take notes. Your GP, your specialist nurse or a UK cancer support line can offer support, and it is fine to ask them to explain things more than once.

If you feel unwell or have urgent symptoms, contact your treating team or NHS 111 / 999. Use 111 for urgent advice and call 999 in an emergency. Do not wait for a second opinion before seeking help when you need it.
