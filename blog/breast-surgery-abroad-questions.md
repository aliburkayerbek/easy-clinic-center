Breast surgery, whether augmentation, reduction, lift or reconstruction, is a significant operation, and doing it in another country adds practical questions on top of the medical ones. This guide is a cautious checklist of questions to put to the surgeon and the hospital before you commit. It does not say which operation suits you, and it makes no promises about results, because those depend on your health, your anatomy and your surgeon's judgement.

## Key takeaways

- Ask who exactly will operate, in which hospital, and get the answers in writing.
- Ask what will be recorded about any implant or device, and keep those details for life.
- Insist on a written plan and a written quote that states what is and is not included.
- Know before you travel who will deal with questions and problems once you are home.
- Keep your expectations realistic and ask the surgeon to explain the limits of what the operation can do.
- A second opinion can help you check the plan before you decide.

## Questions about the surgeon and the hospital

Start with the people and the place. You are entitled to clear answers, and a reputable team will not mind being asked.

- Who will perform my operation, and will I speak to that person before the day of surgery?
- What is their training and specialty, and how often do they perform this procedure?
- In which hospital will the operation take place, and who is the anaesthetist?
- Will the surgeon examine me and discuss my medical history, or is the plan made from photographs alone?
- What happens if the surgeon decides during the consultation that the operation is not right for me?

Be wary if you cannot find out who the surgeon is, or if sales staff answer clinical questions. Our guide to [what to check before plastic surgery in Turkey](/blog/plastic-surgery-in-turkey-what-to-check/) goes through this in more detail.

## Implants and the records you should keep

If an implant or other device is involved, information about it matters for the rest of your life. Ask the team to tell you, and to put in writing:

- The manufacturer, the type and the size or volume of the implant.
- The batch or lot number and the date of the operation.
- The side on which each implant was placed and where it sits in the breast.
- Any advice about future check-ups or imaging.

Keep these details with your medical records. They can be important for any doctor who looks after you later, including at the time of a mammogram or if a problem arises years afterwards. Ask also for an operation note and a discharge summary that states what was done. Our article on [how to prepare medical records for a second opinion](/blog/how-to-prepare-medical-records-for-a-second-opinion/) lists the kind of documents worth gathering.

## Get the plan and the quote in writing

A written plan means you can see what was agreed. Before paying a deposit, ask for a document that sets out:

- The exact operation proposed, and any alternatives the surgeon considered.
- The risks the surgeon wants you to understand, explained in plain language.
- The hospital, the anaesthetic arrangements and the planned length of stay.
- What the price includes, such as consultations, tests, medication, dressings, accommodation and transfers, and what it does not.
- What happens if you need further treatment, and who pays for it.
- The terms for cancelling or postponing.

Read the consent form before the day of surgery, not on arrival. If anything differs from what you were told, ask for it to be corrected in writing. For a broader list, see [questions to ask before treatment abroad](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-treatment-abroad/).

## Aftercare and follow-up at home

This is the part most people think about too late. Surgery is one moment; healing and review continue afterwards, mostly after you have returned to the UK.

- Who will review me before I travel home, and who decides when I am fit to fly? Ask for that advice in writing, not as a general rule.
- How do I contact the team after I leave, through which channel, and when will someone reply?
- What should I do if I am worried about a change in the breast, a wound, a temperature or pain? Which signs mean I should seek urgent help?
- Will the team write to my GP, and can they send my records to a UK doctor if needed?
- Is any follow-up included, such as a review by video, and what is charged separately?

Tell your GP about your operation once you are home. If you are unwell and unsure what to do, NHS 111 can advise, and in an emergency call 999 or go to A&E. Many standard travel insurance policies exclude planned treatment abroad and related complications, so read the terms and speak to your insurer. Our guide to [aftercare when having surgery abroad](/blog/aftercare-when-having-surgery-abroad/) covers this in more depth.

## Realistic expectations

Photographs of other patients show what was possible for them, not what will happen for you. Ask the surgeon to explain what the operation can and cannot change, how your own anatomy affects the options, and what a further procedure might involve in future. A thoughtful surgeon will talk about limits and trade-offs and will not push you to decide quickly. If you feel rushed, that is a reason to pause.
