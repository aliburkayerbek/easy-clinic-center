If you are planning treatment overseas, your medical records are the starting point for everything. They help a doctor understand your history, judge whether a treatment is suitable and plan your care safely. Many UK patients wonder whether they need to translate medical records for treatment abroad, and if so, which ones. This article gives practical guidance. It does not describe any legal requirement, because rules differ between hospitals and countries, and we do not want to guess.

## Key takeaways

- Records in clear English are often usable, but always ask the hospital what it accepts before you spend money on translation.
- Translations help most for the documents that matter to your decision: reports, summaries, results and referral letters.
- Keep your originals and send copies, and keep a record of what you sent and to whom.
- Accuracy matters, so check translated names, dates, doses and test values carefully.
- Send records only through secure channels, and only to people you have chosen to share them with.

## Why translations can help

Doctors reading your records need to understand them quickly and correctly. A misread date, dose or diagnosis can slow down the process or, worse, lead to confusion. Translation can help in several ways:

- It makes your history easier for the treating team to read, particularly if they are working from a mix of handwritten notes and typed letters.
- It reduces the risk of misunderstanding medical terms that are written in abbreviations common in UK practice.

That said, translation is not always necessary. Many specialists working with international patients are comfortable reading English medical documents. The most useful step is simply to ask.

## Ask first: does the hospital accept English records?

Before commissioning any translation, ask the hospital or your coordinator:

- Do you accept my records in English as they are?
- Which documents do you actually need to see?
- Do you need translations of anything, and in what language?
- Do you require any particular format, such as PDF, and is there a size limit?
- Will the treating doctor read the records before giving a plan or quote?

Do not assume there is a rule. Requirements can vary from one hospital to another and from one case to another, and we cannot tell you what any specific hospital will ask for. Get the answer in writing, so you do not pay for work that is not needed. If a translation is requested, ask whether it needs to be done by a particular type of translator or whether a clear, accurate translation is enough.

## Which documents to prepare

You do not usually need to translate everything in your medical file. Focus on what the doctor needs to understand your case. Depending on your situation, these may include:

- **Clinic or discharge letters** that summarise your diagnosis and past treatment.
- **Referral letters** from your GP or specialist.
- **Test results and reports**, such as blood tests, scan reports and pathology results.
- **Operation notes**, if you have had surgery before.
- **Current medicines list**, with doses, and a note of allergies.
- **Any images**, such as scans, usually sent as files rather than printed copies.

Our guide on [how to prepare medical records for a second opinion](/blog/how-to-prepare-medical-records-for-a-second-opinion/) explains how to collect and organise these. Put documents in date order, make sure each page is readable and name files clearly so the reviewer can find what they need.

## Keep your originals

In practice:

- Send copies, scans or photographs, not originals, unless someone specifically explains why an original is needed.
- Keep a list of everything you send, with dates and the name of the recipient.
- Store a full set at home, in paper or digital form, and bring a set with you when you travel.
- If you receive translations, keep both the translated version and the source document together, so anyone can compare them.

You can usually request copies of your own records from your GP surgery or hospital, although it may take some time, so start early.

## Accuracy cautions

A translation is only as good as its accuracy. Mistakes in medical records can matter, so take care.

- **Check the details yourself.** Look at names, dates of birth, dates of tests, doses and units. A small error, such as a misplaced decimal point, can change meaning.
- **Be careful with automated tools.** Free online translators can mistranslate medical terms. If you use one for your own understanding, do not treat the output as a reliable record.
- **Ask a professional where it counts.** If a translation is going to be relied upon for clinical decisions, a qualified translator with medical experience is a sensible choice.
- **Do not edit the originals.** If you spot an error in your records, ask the issuing clinic to correct it rather than changing a document yourself.

## Privacy when sending records

Your medical records are sensitive personal data. Before you send anything:

- Check who will see your records and why. Ask the recipient how they store them and how long they keep them.
- Use secure channels where possible, rather than public or shared accounts.
- Send only what is relevant. You do not have to share your whole file.
- Avoid sending records to anyone who contacts you unprompted or whose identity you cannot confirm.
- Keep copies of what you sent, and delete files you no longer need from shared devices.

If you are unsure, ask the recipient to explain how they handle patient information before you share anything. You are entitled to understand what will happen to your data.
