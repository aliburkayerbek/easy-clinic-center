The honest answer is that it depends on what is being reviewed and who is reviewing it. This guide explains what shapes the price in general terms, what you should expect to receive for it, and how to spot offers that are too vague to compare.

## Why there is no single price

A second opinion can be very small or very large. One person may have a single clinic letter and one blood test. Another may have years of notes, several MRI scans and a biopsy report. Asking a specialist to read and think about these two files is not the same amount of work, so it is reasonable that prices differ.

Be cautious of any provider that quotes one flat figure for everything without asking what you have. Equally, be cautious of a provider that will not give you any indication of cost until after you have paid. A clear process is usually a sign of a more careful service.

## What affects the cost

Without comparing any particular provider, these are the general factors that tend to influence what a second opinion costs.

- **Complexity of the case.** A single, well-defined question is quicker to answer than a condition with several overlapping problems.
- **Volume of records.** More reports, scans and test results take longer to review properly.
- **Specialty.** Some fields need a more narrowly trained specialist, and imaging-heavy cases may need extra care.
- **Format.** A written report is one thing. A live video discussion with the physician is an additional service in many arrangements.
- **Urgency.** Faster turnaround can change what is offered, and you should ask whether that applies.
- **Translation or clarification.** If documents are in another language, extra work may be needed.

## What a good report should include

Price only means something if you know what you are getting. A useful written second opinion generally covers:

- **A summary of your case** in the reviewer's own words, so you can check they understood it.
- **The specialty and role of the reviewer**, such as a licensed specialist physician in the relevant field.
- **An assessment of the diagnosis**, including whether the records support it or whether anything is unclear.
- **Comments on the proposed treatment**, including reasonable alternatives where they exist.
- **Gaps in the information**, for example a scan that would normally be expected but is missing.
- **Questions to take back to your own doctor**, written in plain language.
- **Limits of the review**, since an opinion based on records cannot replace an examination.

If a provider cannot tell you whether you will receive a written document at all, treat that as a warning sign.

## How to avoid vague offers

Some offers sound attractive but leave out the details that matter. Before agreeing to anything, ask:

1. **What exactly is included?** A written report, a call, or both?
2. **Who reviews my case?** You should be told the type of specialist, not just "a doctor".
3. **What do you need from me?** See our guide on [how to prepare medical records for a second opinion](/blog/how-to-prepare-medical-records-for-a-second-opinion/) so your file is complete.
4. **Is the price for my case confirmed in writing?** Avoid anything that changes after you have sent your documents.
5. **What happens if something is missing?** A good service tells you rather than guessing.
6. **Is the opinion linked to a sales pitch?** A second opinion should be independent of any sales process. If it is used to push you towards a particular treatment, be careful.

Also watch for promises. No reviewer can guarantee that your diagnosis is wrong, or that a different treatment will work better. A trustworthy service describes what it can do and what it cannot.
